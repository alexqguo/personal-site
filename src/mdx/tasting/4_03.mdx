In this very special installment of the Tasting Atelier, we will be trying **Buckfast Tonic Wine (15% ABV)**, a special import all the way from the UK! Let's get the basic information about the drink out of the way before delving into its history. This is a caffeinated (!!) fortified wine. Right off the bat, I think this drink would be illegal in the US. As Uncle Ben taught us all, with great power comes great responsibility, and Four Loko taught us that Americans simply cannot handle this responsibility.

There are two versions of this drink produced today: the UK's green bottle version which we will be trying, and Ireland's brown bottle version. Though there are some other smaller differences, notably the green bottle version contains vanilla and roughly half the amount of caffeine of the brown bottle version. A single 750ml bottle of the lesser caffeinated green bottle version contains roughly **seven** soda cans' worth of caffeine. The clerk in the store I bought the bottle at literally laughed at me as I was checking out.

Before exploring the deeper history of this drink, I'm defining a few important terms up front so those like myself who are not familiar with Scottish culture can follow along a bit more easily.

- Benedictine: Relating to Benedict (person, not eggs)
- Benedictine monks: A religious order of Christian monks who follow the "Rule of Saint Benedict"
- Abbey: The building(s) where monks or nuns live
- Ned: A derogatory Scottish term for "a young working-class male who dresses in casual sports clothes". More casually this can also mean an uneducated ruffian, or "non-educated delinquent" similar to a "yob"
  - Fun fact, "ned" was added to the Collins English Dictionary in 2005
- Yob: A coarse and aggressive young person, typically a man. Apparently Scottish men really need to get their shit together!

As mentioned above, Buckfast Tonic Wine is a caffeinated fortified wine. Originally sold as literal medicine (hence "tonic" in the name) by Benedictine monks at Buckfast Abbey in Devon, England in the 1890s, it carried the slogan "Three small glasses a day, for good health and lively blood" and was sold through chemists' shops. 

However after The Great War, stricter licensing laws made it more difficult for the monks to sell their beverage. While selling off their remaining stock, a chance encounter with the vacationing Robert Joyce of the London based wine company J. Chandler & Co Ltd. brought new opportunity. Joyce inquired with the monks as to why they would no longer be selling their product, and after some negotiation he and the monks made a joint decision to reformulate the tonic wine and sell it under his company in 1927.

Buckfast Tonic Wine went on to become one of the best known tonic wines on the market over the next few decades, despite not having any actual active medicinal ingredients. The advertising pushed the medicinal narrative as well, with taglines such as "quick acting and pleasant restorative", "new lease of life", and "get rid of depression". Which, I mean, okay. It's definitely quick acting if nothing else. However the product predictably became associated with binge drinking and many social debates around the responsibility of J. Chandler & Co and the Buckfast Abbey monks ensued. 

To their credit, it does seem like the advertising for Buckfast was targeting the middle-aged and elderly markets in connection with the drink's so-called medicinal properties. This is in stark contrast to numerous American products of a similar nature which get actively and predatorily marketed towards low-income and underaged drinkers.

Either way, many debates on this drink have taken place over the past few decades. On one hand you have those who campaign against the beverage, citing the fundamental contradiction between moderation and debauchery inherent in the drink, as it is produced by monks. This side also calls out stabbing incidents that have occurred with broken Buckfast bottles and the general anti-social behavior the drink encourages. The prevaling counter argument is that Buckfast is no cheaper than other drinks of a similar strength, and the glass bottles are used in production for both ecological and economic reasons. Incredibly, in one study that was conducted the drinkers of Buckfast themselves strongly opposed a potential change to plastic bottles, noting both the impact it would have on the taste (as the wine is frequently drunk straight from the bottle) and amazingly that plastic bottles would make less effective weapons.

Despite all of this, for many in Scotland the drink is a symbol of the working class and acts as an integral part of Scottish culture. Buckfast Tonic Wine is sold in many countries around the world today.
